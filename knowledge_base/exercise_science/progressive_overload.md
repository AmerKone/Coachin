# Progressive Overload and Recovery

Source: Coachin summary of standard progression principles (ACSM progression models; common double-progression and RPE-based autoregulation practice). General guidance, not individual medical advice.

## What progressive overload means

To keep improving, training must gradually become more demanding. Ways to progress, from most to least common:

1. Add load (weight) while keeping reps in the target range.
2. Add reps at the same load.
3. Add a set (increase weekly volume).
4. Improve technique, range of motion or control (slower lowering phase).
5. Reduce rest time (mainly for conditioning goals).

Change one variable at a time so it is clear what caused progress or fatigue.

## Double progression (recommended default)

Each exercise has a rep range, for example 3 sets of 8-12.

1. Start with a weight you can lift for the bottom of the range (8 reps) at about RPE 7-8.
2. Each session, try to add reps until every set reaches the top of the range (12 reps) with good form.
3. When all sets hit the top of the range at or below the target RPE, increase the weight and drop back to the bottom of the range.

## Load increments

- Upper-body barbell lifts: +1-2.5 kg per step.
- Lower-body barbell lifts (squat, deadlift): +2.5-5 kg per step.
- Dumbbells: move to the next available pair (often +1-2 kg per hand); if the jump is large, add reps first.
- Bodyweight exercises: add reps, then use harder variations (incline push-up to push-up to decline or weighted push-up), slower tempo, pauses or added load.
- Beginners can often add weight every session for the first weeks; intermediates progress weekly or over several weeks.

## RPE-based adjustments

- If a set is much easier than the target RPE (2+ points lower), increase the load next time.
- If a set is harder than the target (RPE 9.5-10 when 8 was planned), keep or reduce the load next time.
- Performance fluctuates day to day with sleep, stress and nutrition; RPE lets the plan adapt.

## Deloads

A deload is a planned lighter week that lets fatigue dissipate while keeping skills and fitness.

- Typical deload: reduce sets by about 40-50% and/or reduce intensity by 1-2 RPE points for one week.
- Common timing: every 4-8 weeks, or earlier when signs of accumulated fatigue appear.
- Signs you need a deload: performance dropping for 2+ sessions in a row, persistent joint aches, poor sleep, low motivation, unusually high RPE for normal weights.

## Plateaus

If an exercise stalls for 2-3 weeks despite good sleep and nutrition:

1. Check recovery first (sleep 7-9 hours, adequate protein and calories).
2. Reduce the load by about 10% and build back up with double progression.
3. Change the rep range (e.g. 5-8 instead of 8-12) or swap to a similar exercise variation for a training block.
4. For lifters in a calorie deficit, maintaining strength is a success; large strength gains are less likely while losing fat.

## Recovery basics

- Sleep: 7-9 hours per night; poor sleep reduces strength, recovery and appetite control.
- Protein and total calories support recovery (see nutrition guidance).
- Muscle soreness (DOMS) 24-72 hours after new or harder training is normal and is not a sign of injury; it fades as the body adapts. Sharp, joint-specific or worsening pain is different (see safety guidance).
- Light activity such as walking on rest days can help recovery.
