# Program Design Fundamentals

Source: Coachin summary of widely accepted resistance-training principles (ACSM position stand on progression models in resistance training; NSCA program design guidance). General guidance, not individual medical advice.

## Training frequency and splits

- Beginners (less than ~6 months of consistent training): 2-3 full-body sessions per week, with at least one rest day between sessions. Full-body training lets beginners practise each movement pattern often, which speeds up skill learning.
- Intermediate lifters: 3-5 sessions per week. Common splits are upper/lower (4 days), full-body (3 days) or push/pull/legs (3 or 6 days).
- Advanced lifters: 4-6 sessions per week with more specialised splits, but only if recovery (sleep, nutrition, stress) supports it.
- Train each major muscle group at least twice per week for best results in strength and muscle growth.
- Avoid training the same muscles hard on consecutive days; allow roughly 48 hours before loading a muscle group heavily again.

## Weekly volume

Volume is counted as hard sets per muscle group per week (sets taken within about 0-4 reps of failure).

- Beginners: about 6-10 hard sets per major muscle per week is enough to make excellent progress.
- Intermediate and advanced: about 10-20 hard sets per muscle per week. Start at the lower end and add sets only if progress stalls and recovery is good.
- More is not always better: excessive volume increases soreness, joint stress and injury risk without extra benefit.

## Intensity: RPE and reps in reserve

Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) on a 1-10 scale describes how hard a set felt:

- RPE 10: no more reps possible.
- RPE 9: 1 rep left in reserve.
- RPE 8: 2 reps left in reserve.
- RPE 7: 3 reps left in reserve; the bar speed is still good.
- RPE 6 or below: warm-up or technique work.

Beginners should mostly train at RPE 6-8 while learning technique. Intermediate and advanced lifters typically work at RPE 7-9 on working sets. Training to true failure (RPE 10) is rarely needed and is best limited to the last set of safe isolation exercises.

## Rep ranges by goal

- Strength: 3-6 reps on main compound lifts at higher loads (about 80-90% of one-rep max), with longer rest.
- Muscle gain (hypertrophy): 6-12 reps for most exercises, 10-15 (up to 20-30) for isolation exercises. Muscle growth happens across a wide rep range when sets are taken close to failure.
- Muscular endurance: 12-20+ reps with short rest.
- Fat loss: resistance training preserves muscle during a calorie deficit; use hypertrophy-style training (6-15 reps) plus conditioning. Calorie intake drives fat loss more than rep range.
- General fitness: mix of 6-15 reps across compound and accessory work, plus cardio.

## Exercise selection and order

- Build each session around compound movement patterns: squat, hinge (deadlift variations), horizontal push, horizontal pull, vertical push, vertical pull, and carry/core.
- Order: most technical and heaviest compound lifts first when fresh, then secondary compounds, then isolation work, then core and conditioning.
- Balance pushing and pulling volume to support shoulder health; many programs include slightly more pulling than pushing.
- Choose exercises the person can perform with good technique and without pain; there is always an alternative for any movement pattern.

## Rest periods

- Heavy compound lifts (strength focus): 2-5 minutes.
- Hypertrophy compounds: 1.5-3 minutes.
- Isolation and accessory exercises: 60-90 seconds.
- Conditioning and circuits: 30-60 seconds.
Shorter rest reduces the load you can lift; for strength and muscle gain it is better to rest enough to maintain performance.

## Session length and structure

- Budget roughly 2-3 minutes per working set including rest when fitting a program to a time limit.
- Typical structure: 5-10 minute warm-up (light cardio, dynamic mobility, lighter ramp-up sets of the first exercise), main work, optional accessories, cool-down.
- A 45-60 minute session usually fits 4-6 exercises and 12-20 working sets.

## Beginner priorities

1. Learn technique on a small set of fundamental movements.
2. Train consistently for months; adherence beats program optimisation.
3. Progress gradually (see progressive overload guidance).
4. Keep most sets at RPE 6-8 and avoid training to failure on complex lifts.
